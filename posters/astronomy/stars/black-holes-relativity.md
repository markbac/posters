## Introduction {.abstract}

In 1915 Einstein replaced Newton's pull of gravity with **general relativity**: matter and energy tell spacetime how to curve, and curved spacetime tells matter and light how to move. Pack enough mass into a small enough space and the escape speed passes the **speed of light**. Nothing, not even light, comes back across that boundary: it is a **black hole**.

## 1. The size of a black hole

```equation
title: Schwarzschild radius
R_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2} \approx 2.95 \text{ km} \times \frac{M}{M_\odot}
R_s | Radius of the event horizon | m
G | Gravitational constant |
M | Mass of the black hole | kg
c | Speed of light | m/s
```

| Compressed into a black hole | Its event horizon |
|---|---|
| Earth | 9 mm: a marble |
| The Sun | 3 km |
| Sagittarius A*, 4.3 million Suns | 12.7 million km: 18 Suns wide |
| M87*, 6.5 billion Suns | 19 billion km: three times Pluto's orbit |

## 2. Around a spinning black hole

```cards cols=1 style=label
Singularity | Where general relativity breaks down. In a spinning (Kerr) black hole, a ring
Event horizon, 1 Rs | Escape speed equals c: the point of no return
Photon sphere, 1.5 Rs | Light can circle the hole: the bright ring in the EHT images
Innermost stable orbit, 3 Rs | Inside this, matter plunges. Outside it, the accretion disk glows in X-rays at 10 million K
Jets | Twisted magnetic fields launch plasma along the poles at over 99 % of c
```

Infalling matter can give out **up to 42 %** of its rest mass as radiation, round a fast-spinning hole. Fusion gives 0.7 %.

## 3. Gravity bends time

```equation
title: Time dilation outside the horizon
t_{\text{far}} = \frac{t_{\text{near}}}{\sqrt{1 - R_s / r}}
t_{\text{near}} | Time on a clock at distance r |
t_{\text{far}} | The same interval, seen from far away |
r | Distance from the centre | m
```

To a distant watcher, a probe falling in slows, reddens and fades, and never quite crosses. The probe's own clock ticks on normally as it falls through.

## 4. Spaghettification and Hawking radiation

```cards cols=1 style=top
Tides go as 1/r³ | What tears you apart is the **difference** in gravity between head and feet. Near a 10-Sun hole that difference is huge outside the horizon; near M87* it is negligible | purple
Hawking radiation | Quantum effects at the horizon make a black hole glow, at a temperature that goes as 1/M. Real black holes are colder than the 2.7 K CMB and would take 10⁶⁷ to 10¹⁰⁰ years to evaporate | blue
```

## 5. Three kinds of black hole {.wide}

```compare
| Stellar-mass | Intermediate-mass | Supermassive
Mass | **3 to about 100 Suns** | **100 to 100,000 Suns** | **A million to 40 billion Suns**
Horizon across | 18 to 600 km | 600 to 600,000 km | **The size of a solar system**
How they form | The collapse of a star over about 25 Suns | Mergers in dense star clusters, such as GW190521 | Seeds that grew for billions of years, at the centre of every large galaxy
Tides at the horizon | **Deadly**: a person is stretched apart before reaching it | Strong | **Gentle**: you could cross M87*'s horizon and feel nothing
Examples | **Cygnus X-1**, 21 Suns, the first found | Candidates in Omega Centauri and HLX-1 | **Sagittarius A*** and **M87***, imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope
```

## 6. Hearing black holes collide

Two black holes spiralling together shake spacetime itself. **LIGO** splits a laser down two 4 km arms; a passing wave stretches one and squeezes the other, and the light interferes differently.

## 8. Gravitational-wave observatories {style=card colour=purple}

```cards cols=1 style=label
LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA | 10 Hz to 10 kHz: stellar-mass black holes and neutron stars merging
LISA | From the late 2030s: three spacecraft 2.5 million km apart, for supermassive black holes merging
Pulsar timing arrays | Nanohertz: a background hum, found in 2023, likely from pairs of supermassive black holes
Event Horizon Telescope | Not waves but light: radio dishes across Earth that imaged M87* and Sgr A*
```

## In short {.summary}

A black hole is curved spacetime where gravity beats every other force. Feeding black holes are the brightest engines in the universe, clocks slow near their horizons, and laser interferometers hear them merge.

### Gravity is geometry

Planets and light follow the straightest paths through curved spacetime.

### Not cosmic vacuum cleaners

Swap the Sun for a black hole of the same mass, 6 km across, and Earth would keep the same orbit, in the dark.

### Spacetime is stiff

Two black holes of 30 Suns colliding moved LIGO's mirrors by a thousandth of a proton's width.
