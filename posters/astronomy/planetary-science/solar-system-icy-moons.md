## Introduction {.abstract}

**4.567 billion years ago** a cold cloud of gas and dust collapsed, perhaps pushed by a nearby supernova. **99.86 %** of its mass fell to the centre and became the **Sun**; the rest flattened into a disk. One boundary in that disk, the **frost line**, explains why the inner planets are small and rocky and the outer ones giants, with moons that hide **oceans**.

## 1. The Solar System on a log scale {.wide}

Inside the frost line only rock and metal, half a percent of the nebula, could freeze into grains. Beyond it, below 170 K, ices froze too, and there was four times as much solid matter. Cores of ten Earths formed fast enough there to pull in hydrogen and helium before the gas cleared.

## 3. When the giants wandered

```cards cols=1 style=top
The Grand Tack | In the first few million years, Jupiter may have drifted in to 1.5 AU, where Mars is now, and starved Mars of material. When Saturn caught up and locked into a 3:2 resonance, both turned back out, like a boat tacking | amber
The Nice model | Uranus and Neptune formed closer in. Tugs from Jupiter and Saturn later destabilised them: Neptune was thrown out to 30 AU, scattering the Kuiper Belt into the Oort Cloud and showering the inner planets with debris | blue
```

## 4. How a moon keeps an ocean warm

Io, Europa and Ganymede orbit in 1.77, 3.55 and 7.15 days, a 1:2:4 **resonance**. Their tugs keep each orbit slightly oval, so Jupiter stretches and squeezes them as they go round: Europa flexes by about 30 m every orbit, and the friction heats it.

## In short {.summary}

The frost line split the young Solar System into rocky inner planets and giant outer ones. Moving giants then shaped the belts and the Oort Cloud. Today, tidal heating keeps oceans liquid under the ice of moons far from the Sun.

### Why Jupiter is so big

Just outside the frost line, it had first pick of the ice, and grew a core fast enough to gather 300 Earths of gas.

### Saturn's rings may be young

Cassini found them clean and bright, perhaps a few hundred million years old: a moon torn apart.

### Free ocean samples

Enceladus sprays its ocean into space. A spacecraft only has to fly through.
